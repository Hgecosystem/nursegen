Aged Care Recruitment

Why Good Aged Care Candidates Drop Out of Recruitment Processes

NurseGen·14 Aug 2026·7 min read

Finding a strong candidate is only half the recruitment challenge.

In aged care, employers are often competing for experienced Registered Nurses, clinical leaders, managers and other skilled professionals. When a good candidate enters your recruitment process, there is a strong chance yours isn’t the only opportunity they’re considering.

Yet organisations can spend considerable time and resources attracting candidates, only to lose them somewhere between application and offer.

Sometimes the candidate accepts another position. Sometimes they stop responding. Sometimes they withdraw after an interview. And sometimes they make it all the way to offer stage before deciding the opportunity isn’t right for them.

When this happens regularly, the answer isn’t always to generate more applications.

It may be worth looking at what candidates experience once they enter your recruitment process.

1. The recruitment process takes too long

One of the simplest reasons good candidates disappear is also one of the most common: someone else gets there first.

Consider the number of steps that can sit between application and employment: application → screening → interview → feedback → references → approval → offer → pre-employment requirements → commencement.

Every stage is important, but the gaps between them can quickly add up.

A CV sits waiting to be reviewed. It takes several days to coordinate an interview. Feedback isn’t provided immediately. References aren’t requested until later in the process. An offer requires another level of approval.

None of these delays may seem significant individually. Together, they can turn what should be a relatively efficient process into several weeks.

Meanwhile, the candidate is still looking for work.

Recruitment speed doesn’t mean rushing decisions or compromising your standards. It means identifying unnecessary delays and making sure the important parts of your recruitment process happen efficiently.

2. Communication disappears between stages

Candidates don’t necessarily expect an immediate answer after every interaction.

They do, however, want to know what’s happening.

One of the easiest ways to create uncertainty is to leave a candidate wondering whether they’re still being considered.

A simple update can make a significant difference: “We’re still completing interviews and expect to have an update for you by Friday.”

It doesn’t promise an outcome. It simply keeps the candidate informed.

Without communication, candidates are left to make their own assumptions. They may conclude the organisation isn’t interested. They may question how organised the workplace is. Or they may simply focus their attention on another employer who is communicating more consistently.

Candidate communication doesn’t need to be complicated. It needs to be timely, clear and genuine.

3. Salary expectations aren’t addressed early enough

Remuneration conversations can sometimes be treated as something to deal with towards the end of recruitment.

That can create problems.

If a candidate progresses through screening, interviews and references only to discover the salary isn’t aligned with their expectations, both parties have invested significant time in a process that was unlikely to result in a successful hire.

Where possible, provide candidates with enough information early to determine whether the opportunity is financially suitable. That might include an hourly rate, salary range, applicable Enterprise Agreement, Award conditions or other relevant remuneration information.

Transparency doesn’t guarantee a candidate will accept. It does help ensure everyone is having the same conversation from the beginning.

4. The roster isn’t what the candidate expected

In aged care, salary is only part of the employment decision.

The roster can be equally important.

A candidate may be interested in the organisation and the position but unable or unwilling to work the roster required.

Consider the difference between “must have flexible availability” and “this is a permanent full-time position working a rotating roster, including every second weekend.”

The second statement gives the candidate something concrete to assess.

Permanent nights, rotating shifts, weekend requirements, Monday-to-Friday positions and fixed part-time arrangements appeal to different candidates.

Being clear about roster expectations early can reduce withdrawals later.

5. The candidate doesn’t get a clear picture of the role

A position description tells someone what they’re responsible for. It doesn’t always tell them what the job will actually feel like.

Experienced aged care candidates often want to understand more.

  • What is the team structure?
  • Who will they report to?
  • What clinical support is available?
  • What systems are used?
  • What are the priorities for the role?
  • Why is the position vacant?
  • What are the challenges?
  • What would success look like in the first three or six months?

This is particularly important when recruiting senior clinical and operational positions.

Candidates don’t expect every workplace to be perfect. In many cases, experienced professionals appreciate a realistic conversation about both the opportunity and the challenges.

The objective isn’t to make every vacancy sound easy. It’s to help candidates make an informed decision about whether it’s right for them.

6. The interview feels like a one-way assessment

Interviews naturally involve assessing whether someone has the experience, skills and approach required for the position.

But there’s another interview happening at the same time.

The candidate is interviewing you.

They’re noticing whether the interviewer was prepared. They’re listening to how the organisation talks about its employees. They’re assessing the leadership style. They’re considering whether expectations seem realistic. They’re deciding whether they can picture themselves working there.

This is particularly important when you’re interviewing an experienced candidate who may have several employment options.

An effective interview should give both sides enough information to make a good decision.

7. There’s no compelling reason to choose the organisation

Aged care job advertisements often do an excellent job of explaining what employers require: qualifications, experience, registration, police checks, availability, systems knowledge, leadership capability.

But there’s sometimes much less information explaining what the candidate receives in return.

When someone has multiple opportunities, they need a reason to choose one over another. And that reason doesn’t always need to be salary.

It might be a highly regarded Facility Manager, a stable clinical team, supportive leadership, predictable rostering, professional development, career progression, strong clinical systems, flexibility, an attractive regional lifestyle, or the opportunity to make a meaningful contribution during a period of change.

The important question is: what genuinely makes this opportunity worth choosing?

If your recruitment team can’t clearly answer that question, candidates may struggle to answer it too.

8. The process feels different from the promise

Candidate experience begins well before someone’s first day.

If your advertisement talks about communication, respect, teamwork and a people-first culture, candidates will naturally notice whether those values are reflected during recruitment.

  • Were they welcomed?
  • Was the interview organised?
  • Were expectations clear?
  • Did people follow through when they said they would?
  • Were questions answered openly?
  • Did they receive updates?

Small interactions contribute to a candidate’s perception of the organisation.

Recruitment is one of the first opportunities an employer has to demonstrate its culture rather than simply describe it.

Before looking for more candidates, look at the process

When recruitment becomes difficult, the natural response is often: we need more applicants.

Sometimes that’s absolutely true.

But if suitable candidates are already entering the process and repeatedly withdrawing, declining offers or disappearing, increasing applicant numbers may not solve the underlying problem.

Instead, map your recruitment process from the candidate’s perspective. Ask:

  • How quickly do we respond?
  • Where do candidates wait?
  • Are salary and roster expectations discussed early?
  • Do candidates understand the opportunity?
  • Are we communicating consistently?
  • Can we clearly explain why someone should choose us?
  • How long does it take us to make an offer once we’ve identified the right person?

The answers may reveal opportunities to improve recruitment without necessarily increasing advertising or sourcing activity.

Good recruitment isn’t only about attraction

Attracting good people matters.

But so does what happens next.

A strong recruitment process should help the organisation make an informed hiring decision while giving the candidate the information and confidence they need to make their own.

In a competitive aged care workforce market, organisations can’t control how many other opportunities a candidate may have.

They can control the experience they provide.

And sometimes, the difference between securing a great candidate and starting the search again isn’t finding more people.

It’s making it easier for the right person to say yes.

Better workforce. Stronger care.
